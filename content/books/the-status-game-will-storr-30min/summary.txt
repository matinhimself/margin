The Status Game: On Social Position and How We Use It
Will Storr

Independent extended summary
30 minutes at 200 words per minute

An independently written extended summary of the supplied 2021 William Collins ebook, not an author-endorsed condensation. Will Storr argues that our pursuit of belonging and respect helps explain cooperation, ambition, conformity, conflict, and identity. This edition develops his examples in greater depth, from Ben Gunn’s prison identity and organisational incentives to belief communities, coercive movements, and the rise of knowledge-based success. It distinguishes his three routes to status, preserves qualifications around his psychological and historical interpretations, and ends with his seven practical recommendations. The focus is on choosing worthwhile commitments, recognising distorted incentives, and creating respect without becoming dependent on one group’s approval.

Belonging and respect answer different needs

Will Storr’s central argument is that much of human life becomes easier to understand when we see people seeking both membership and standing. We want to belong to groups, but we also want to count for something within them. Acceptance answers the question of whether we are included. Status answers the question of how much others value our presence, abilities, and judgement.

Status is therefore broader than money, celebrity, or an impressive job. It appears whenever someone treats us as worth listening to, seeks our advice, admires our contribution, or allows us to influence a decision. These moments can be small and fleeting. A person can lack public distinction while being deeply respected in a family, workshop, sports team, or neighbourhood. Conversely, someone with an imposing title can feel ignored or despised by the people whose opinions matter most.

Storr grounds this sensitivity in our evolutionary dependence on cooperative groups. In his account, our ancestors needed allies and shared resources to survive. Being useful and respected improved access to protection, food, and opportunities to reproduce. The modern settings are different, but the desire to secure a worthwhile place remains. Approval can bring pleasure and purpose; rejection and loss of standing can feel disproportionately threatening.

The research he discusses links social connection and perceived rank with wellbeing, physical health, and vulnerability to depression. These findings support the importance of social life without making status a complete explanation of illness or distress. His larger point is that respect is a human need with substantial consequences. Treating it as mere vanity leaves us poorly equipped to understand either our own reactions or the behaviour of other people. The task is to recognise the need and examine the ways we try to meet it.

Meaning is often built inside a local world

The opening account of Ben Gunn makes status concrete. Imprisoned after killing another child when he was fourteen, Gunn initially experienced extreme degradation and despair. Over time, he educated himself and developed an identity around challenging abuses of prison authority. He became knowledgeable about the system and useful to fellow prisoners who wanted to contest its decisions.

From outside, release might seem an uncomplicated improvement. It promised freedom, love, and a more comfortable life. Yet Gunn resisted opportunities to leave. Inside prison, he understood the hierarchy and occupied a recognisable place as a lifer and an advocate. Outside it, much of the knowledge and reputation he had accumulated would cease to carry the same value. A seemingly inferior environment had become the setting in which his life made sense.

Storr does not present this as a reason to prefer prison. He uses it to challenge the assumption that wellbeing follows automatically from better external circumstances. People also need a role in which their efforts are intelligible and their contribution is recognised. Leaving a difficult situation may involve losing that role before another can be built. Gunn’s eventual public writing offered a possible bridge, extending his identity beyond the prison audience.

The story introduces the importance of reference groups. We do not experience rank only against an abstract scale of all humanity. We compare ourselves within particular communities, using standards we have learned to care about. Moving between those communities can transform the value of the same qualities. Storr’s account suggests why a transition that looks like advancement can still unsettle identity. It also shows that meaningful recognition can be constructed under severely constrained circumstances, because usefulness and respect are not limited to conventional forms of success.

The mind tells a flattering story about the contest

We rarely experience ourselves as organisms trying to improve social rank. We experience ourselves as central characters pursuing justified goals. Storr argues that the conscious mind makes a persuasive story out of events, often casting our intentions generously and explaining our difficulties through other people’s failings. We see ambition and vanity readily in rivals while overlooking their presence in ourselves.

This helps explain why admitting a desire for status can feel awkward. Acknowledging the motive may itself lower our standing, suggesting insecurity or calculation. The mind has reasons to keep it partly hidden. A moral dispute can consequently feel like pure devotion to truth even when the approval of an audience is helping to sustain our anger.

Status also works through symbols. Clothes, possessions, titles, accents, eye contact, tone of voice, and conversational attention can convey where someone stands. We learn to read these signals quickly and often unconsciously. Their meaning depends on the setting: what impresses one group may seem pretentious, irrelevant, or discreditable to another. Storr’s examples range from luxury objects to unusually large yams, making familiar ambitions look less inevitable.


Comparison intensifies the effect. Money provides material benefits, but it can also tell us how we are doing relative to other people. An increase may feel less satisfying if the people around us have advanced further. Storr discusses disagreements within happiness research rather than claiming that income never matters. His argument is that the symbolic and comparative aspects are easy to underestimate.

Becoming aware of this machinery does not allow us to stand wholly outside it. It gives us a pause in which to examine an interpretation. Before treating a slight as proof of another person’s wickedness, we can notice whether the incident has threatened our sense of importance and encouraged a conveniently flattering story.

Cultures teach us what deserves recognition

The appetite for respect does not dictate one universal definition of a good life. Groups supply rules and symbols that translate it into particular ambitions. They tell members which achievements count, what politeness requires, who should defer to whom, and which violations deserve embarrassment or punishment. Much of this instruction happens without anyone formally announcing the rules.

Storr illustrates the process with the English pub. Where conversation with a stranger is welcome, how a person attracts the bartender’s attention, and how an invisible queue is respected all involve learned conventions. Insiders experience the behaviour as ordinary. An outsider may break a rule without knowing one exists, then encounter a judgement that seems puzzling or unfair.

Differences can become more consequential across cultures. Some settings place greater weight on individual distinction; others place greater weight on maintaining the group’s standing and harmony. Praise directed at one person can therefore have different effects. Storr presents broad patterns rather than a fixed division between every Western and East Asian individual. The essential point is that a strategy learned in one environment may fail in another.

These standards help form the self. Children learn through imitation, correction, pride, and shame. Adolescents become especially sensitive to their peers and begin to build identities around new communities. A conscience develops partly through internalising the audience’s expectations, so that imagined approval and disapproval can guide behaviour even when nobody is watching.

The available games are also constrained by personality and circumstance. Education, family resources, temperament, and social connections influence which groups we can enter and how successfully we can participate. Storr challenges the promise that determination alone can make anyone anything. Understanding the rules of a setting should include understanding its barriers, rather than interpreting every difficulty as evidence of personal failure.

Dominance virtue and success are three overlapping strategies

Storr organises status seeking around three routes. Dominance produces submission through force or fear. Virtue earns recognition for behaviour the group regards as moral, loyal, courageous, generous, or dutiful. Success earns recognition through skill, knowledge, and achievement against specified standards. A threatening bully, a celebrated contributor to a community, and an outstanding craftsperson rise through different means.

Virtue and success belong to the broader category of prestige. People grant prestige because they see the recipient as valuable. Someone may possess useful expertise or demonstrate a commitment to the collective good. The desire for recognition can thus support cooperation. A hunter can gain esteem by obtaining food, while sharing that food generously can bring another kind of esteem. Self-interest and benefits to others need not move in opposite directions.

The categories are not boxes into which every organisation or individual fits. Storr emphasises that actual games blend them. A sporting contest depends on skill, but also on rules and sometimes intimidation. A business rewards performance, promotes its moral commitments, and may use legal or organisational power against opponents. One mode can dominate without eliminating the others.

The same flexibility exists within people. We may earn respect through competent work, display loyalty among friends, and then demand submission in an argument. Seeing ourselves as fundamentally good or successful does not prevent a shift into dominance. Storr’s framework asks what strategy is operating in a particular encounter and what the surrounding group encourages.

This distinction is useful because not all increases in influence have the same consequences. A person can gain compliance while losing freely offered admiration. Another can become more influential because others genuinely want to learn from them. Understanding the difference helps explain why winning an immediate contest can damage a relationship, while making oneself useful can create standing that other people willingly sustain.

Prestige helps knowledge spread and can spread error too

Prestige gives people a reason to pay attention to the capable. If someone succeeds at tasks our community values, learning from them may help us succeed as well. In Storr’s account, this contributes to the extraordinary human capacity to accumulate and transmit culture. Individuals need not discover every useful technique afresh; they can inherit successful practices from others.

Our copying is often expansive, however. We imitate admired people’s speech, clothes, tastes, and convictions alongside their relevant expertise. Some of this happens without conscious calculation. Storr describes cues that draw our attention: similarity to ourselves, apparent skill, signs of success, and evidence that other people already defer to the person. These shortcuts are understandable but imperfect.

For example, a symbol of success can be easier to notice than the underlying ability. Likewise, attention from an audience can attract still more attention. Someone may become influential partly because others appear to regard them as influential. The mechanism that helps us find useful teachers can elevate a person well beyond the domain in which they have demonstrated competence.

Reputation is central here. Our social standing exists in other people’s partial representations of us, shaped by what they witness and hear. Gossip communicates both those representations and the group’s standards: stories of admiration and disgrace teach listeners what earns respect and what puts membership at risk. It can reward contribution and restrain abuse, but it can also distort, exclude, and discredit.

Storr’s account invites caution about transferring trust from one area to another. Someone’s genuine achievement does not make every opinion equally reliable. Equally, a reputation is never a complete account of a person. We interact through selective social impressions, and those impressions are influenced by the interests, expectations, and status concerns of the people who carry them.

Dominance tries to force the recognition it cannot earn

Dominance is visible in physical aggression, but it also appears in smaller attempts to impose rank. A demand for special treatment, a threatening posture, or a humiliating rebuke can try to make another person acknowledge our importance. Storr’s example of Caren Turner confronting police officers illustrates how credentials and authority can become tools in a struggle for deference.

Such encounters reveal a distinction between being obeyed and being esteemed. A display of power may obtain a short-term result while causing others to lower their opinion of the person using it. We can shift into this mode when a minor inconvenience feels like disrespect. The practical problem is then no longer only the delayed service or unanswered question; it becomes a contest over who is entitled to control the interaction.

Humiliation is a particularly severe form of status injury. It involves being made to feel degraded in other people’s eyes. Storr examines individuals whose histories combine rejection with an unusually grand conviction that they deserve superiority. In his reading, violence can become an attempt to reverse unbearable inferiority and impose the recognition they believe has been wrongly withheld.

His discussion of Elliot Rodger is an extreme example. Rodger’s self-explanations converted loneliness and rejection into a story of entitlement, enemies, and supposedly justified revenge. Storr uses it to examine a dangerous combination of wounded standing and grandiosity. It is not an explanation of every violent act, and it does not mean rejection ordinarily produces violence. Many factors shape such outcomes.

The wider lesson is to notice how easily a threatened self can generate an accusation that feels morally certain. Explanation does not excuse harm. It reveals why protecting dignity and avoiding needless humiliation can matter, and why attempts to obtain respect through coercion often create damage far beyond the original slight.

Organisations shape character through their daily rewards

Storr argues that a group does more than assemble people with existing qualities. Its reward system can change what they do and what they learn to regard as acceptable. Members watch how recognition, security, and advancement are distributed. Repeatedly rewarded conduct starts to appear sensible, while conduct that carries a penalty becomes harder to sustain.

His account of professional life describes the gradual corruption of young lawyers. Under pressure to perform, impress senior colleagues, and maintain an expensive image of success, small billing distortions become tempting. A person can initially promise to compensate a client later, then become accustomed to the extra charge. Each step has a local justification. The change in character need not begin with a dramatic decision to become dishonest.

Enron provides an organisational example. Its intense internal ranking made people’s relative position unusually consequential. Colleagues could become direct threats to one another’s security and advancement. Competition can increase effort, but severe internal rivalry can discourage cooperation and create incentives to protect appearances rather than reveal problems. Storr treats this culture as part of the company’s corruption, not a complete explanation of its failure.

Rivalry itself can also energise useful achievement. Long-running opponents care strongly about defeating one another, and that concern can stimulate effort and innovation. The question is how the energy is directed. A common external challenge can unite a team; a system that makes internal defeat constantly threatening can encourage secrecy, hostility, and cheating.

A useful implication of the book is to inspect an organisation’s actual rewards. Does it honour shared success, honest reporting, and competence, or mainly visible victories and loyalty to powerful people? Values written on a wall cannot settle the question. The recurring consequences of everyday behaviour reveal the rules members are really learning to follow.

Social media makes recognition measurable and intermittent

Social media concentrates familiar social motives into a highly accessible environment. People display achievements, signal commitments, learn from admired figures, compete for attention, and punish perceived violations. Storr sees the same three strategies at work online: success, virtue, and dominance. The setting is new, but the need for an audience’s recognition is familiar.

The design adds important pressures. Likes, follower counts, and visible engagement turn ambiguous approval into apparent scores. A person can repeatedly check whether their standing has risen and compare their own response with the attention others receive. The audience also grows far beyond the small communities for which much of our social sensitivity developed.

Storr discusses persuasive technology and the use of unpredictable reinforcement. The next check may produce an exciting response, or it may produce little. Not knowing makes another check tempting. He describes his own habitual phone use to show that insight and relatively restrained posting do not automatically remove the pull. Attention can become organised around the possibility of the next small reward.

These systems also lower the effort needed to join moral disputes. A judgement about a distant person can win immediate recognition from an approving group. Public support may reward courage and useful criticism, but it can also reward exaggeration, aggression, or conspicuous certainty. What repeatedly earns attention becomes a lesson about how to behave.

The book’s perspective encourages us to examine both personal habits and the surrounding incentives. Compulsive checking is not only a mysterious individual weakness; it takes place inside a system that makes social rewards frequent and variable. Likewise, online conduct is easier to understand when we ask who is supplying recognition and what kinds of performance receive it. The resulting picture connects private unease with the public rules of the platform.

More status does not end the desire for status

Storr calls attention to a persistent flaw in the pursuit of recognition: success rarely delivers the permanent satisfaction we imagine. A higher position becomes familiar. A new comparison becomes salient. Because standing depends on other people’s judgements, it can also be withdrawn. We are encouraged to keep accumulating something we cannot finally secure.

Paul McCartney’s concern about the ordering of songwriting credits provides a memorable example. An extraordinary career and enormous admiration did not make that smaller question of recognition irrelevant. Storr’s point is not to ridicule a person for being human. It is that even exceptional acclaim does not reliably eliminate sensitivity to comparative rank. The imagined moment when nobody else’s judgement can trouble us may never arrive.

The same tendency can distort leadership. Subordinates have reasons to praise a powerful person and avoid disagreement. Leaders may then accept the flattering information as a fair assessment of their own judgement. When problems are hidden and dissenters appear difficult or disloyal, the absence of criticism seems to confirm that everything is going well. The resulting confidence becomes less and less connected to reliable feedback.

Storr discusses organisational research in which flattery and habitual agreement are associated with poor decisions and an inability to change course. He also notes the value of followers who are willing to challenge leaders. Respectful disagreement can protect a group from the consequences of its hierarchy rather than undermine the hierarchy’s legitimate purpose.

For an individual, recognising the moving finish line changes the purpose of ambition. It remains possible to value achievement, skill, and contribution. What becomes doubtful is the promise that one promotion, prize, or financial milestone will settle the question of personal worth forever. Storr recommends finding satisfaction in worthwhile movement rather than expecting a final arrival that makes us immune to comparison.

We admire high status and resent it at the same time

Our response to successful people is often divided. We want to learn from them, be near them, or identify with them. We may also enjoy seeing them humbled. Storr traces this ambivalence to the history of human cooperation. Groups needed useful, capable people, but they also needed to restrain anyone who tried to claim too much power or privilege.

He describes relatively egalitarian communities in which conspicuous self-importance is checked through mockery and public disapproval. Shallow hierarchies do not necessarily mean nobody cares about rank. They may be maintained precisely because people resist being dominated. The same sensitivity can appear in modern pleasure at a celebrated person’s embarrassment, especially when the person succeeds in a domain we care about ourselves.

As settled societies grew, wealth and authority could accumulate far beyond the limits of small groups. Titles, offices, and rituals established formal standing. Yet the informal game continued in people’s private judgements. A person could possess authority without admiration, or widespread admiration without equivalent official power. Storr uses a royal example from the book’s own period to show how these two hierarchies can diverge.

That divergence can make leaders anxious. Formal obedience does not tell them whether people genuinely respect them. An insecure leader may demand increasing demonstrations of loyalty while making honest feedback even less likely. Subordinates then learn to perform admiration, deepening the uncertainty the performance was meant to solve.

The distinction helps clarify everyday encounters with hierarchy. Holding a position does not guarantee that others consider its occupant wise or fair. Equally, an absence of grand titles does not mean someone lacks influence. Storr’s account directs attention toward actual deference, learning, and trust, while warning that resentment can turn a reasonable objection to abuse into indiscriminate enjoyment of another person’s fall.

Believing together can become more rewarding than checking

A belief can do two jobs at once. It can explain the world, and it can establish membership in a community. Storr argues that the second job often receives too little attention. Accepting a shared account may bring friendship, purpose, and esteem. Doubting it can threaten all three. What looks like a disagreement over evidence may therefore involve the possible loss of a social world.

Maranda Dynda’s experience in an anti-vaccination community illustrates the attraction. The group offered support and a sense of possessing knowledge that outsiders lacked. Defending its claims became a route to recognition. Carrying the message into other conversations and reporting back could reinforce her place in the group. Storr recounts the mechanism to explain the persistence of false beliefs, not to support the medical claims involved.

He also examines the Satanic Panic, in which allegations of extraordinary ritual abuse acquired authority through professional networks. Therapists, investigators, conferences, publications, and public attention created opportunities for respected roles. Interview methods could reward children for supplying the expected account. The perceived seriousness of the cause helped make doubt look like indifference to victims.

The people involved did not all need to be cynical. Sincere commitment can coexist with incentives that distort judgement. Nor does education remove the problem: prestige may make a weak claim sound credible, while agreement from colleagues makes it feel increasingly secure. Escalating conviction can come to reflect mutual reinforcement rather than improving evidence.

Storr’s broader warning applies to our own most comfortable convictions. We often ask trusted people what to believe because checking every claim independently is impossible. The risk arises when we forget that trust is selective and that belonging makes some conclusions attractive. A useful question is whether we would accept the same quality of evidence from a rival group, and whether our community permits correction without treating the person who changes their mind as an enemy.

Threat can produce a competition in moral purity

When a group feels under attack, members may become more tightly bound to its standards. Storr draws on research into tight and loose cultures to explain the value as well as the cost of strong norms. Coordination and self-control can help people respond to danger. Problems intensify when questioning the group itself becomes a violation that threatens belonging.

Under these conditions, members can earn recognition by demonstrating that they are more committed than others. A harsher judgement or more demanding rule becomes evidence of superior loyalty. Others then face a choice between appearing ordinary and matching the escalation. The group’s position can move toward greater severity without any one person having planned the destination.

Storr describes this as a path toward purity competition. People condemn smaller deviations, scrutinise one another’s language, and look for hidden betrayal. Some members may agree sincerely; others may comply because they fear the consequences of disagreement. Since visible agreement no longer reveals private conviction, suspicion can grow even as conformity increases. Accusing someone else becomes a way to demonstrate one’s own reliability.

The enforcement is not always directed from a powerful leader downward. Peers can impose the rules through gossip, ostracism, and collective punishment. Storr’s discussion of the historical power of clan members and elders connects this horizontal control to contemporary online mobbing. Participation may bring recognition for defending the group, even when the target’s conduct has been interpreted unfairly.

This analysis does not make every moral norm oppressive or every public criticism illegitimate. It identifies warning signs in the way a community handles disagreement. Does it allow proportion, evidence, correction, and uncertainty? Or does it reward denunciation while portraying injury to an opponent as proof of virtue? When loyalty is measured mainly through punishment, the pursuit of moral standing can become an increasingly coercive game.

A monopoly over identity makes a group dangerous

The more exclusively people depend on one community, the harder its claims become to question. Losing membership may mean losing friendships, daily structure, a respected identity, and a picture of the future. Storr argues that extreme groups can exploit this dependence, but their appeal also requires explanation. They offer certainty, belonging, and a clearly marked route toward significance.

His account of Heaven’s Gate shows how ordinary details of life became organised around the group’s beliefs. Members adopted new names, specialised language, schedules, and procedures. Correct behaviour offered evidence of progress toward a promised higher existence. The system reached into private thought, encouraging people to monitor themselves and others. A world that outsiders found bizarre could feel reassuringly meaningful from within.

Storr challenges the idea that describing members as simply brainwashed explains everything. Some actively wanted the discipline because they accepted the goal it supposedly served. That acceptance did not make the beliefs true or the outcome safe. It made them powerful enough to organise choices. When the community’s shared story changed, members could interpret terrible harm through its promise of fulfilment.

He also examines domination at the scale of nations. Accounts of Nazism and Soviet Communism show movements offering restored dignity, new opportunities, or collective salvation while creating coercive hierarchies of their own. The suppression of rivals, denunciation of supposed internal enemies, and removal of independent sources of recognition made dissent increasingly costly.

The Soviet example also challenges the idea that abolishing private ownership would abolish the desire to rise. In Storr’s account, official commitments to equality coexisted with differentiated access to housing, food, education, material goods, and authority. Titles and honours could return under new justifications. Privilege was sometimes defended as access to state property rather than personal ownership, but the distinction did not remove its social meaning. People still competed for valued positions and the benefits attached to them. The lesson concerns the persistence of status across institutional arrangements: eliminating a familiar symbol does not necessarily eliminate the underlying contest, and a promise of equality does not exempt a movement’s actual distribution of power from scrutiny.

These cases differ profoundly in history, scale, and consequences. Storr connects them through particular mechanisms rather than treating every group as equivalent: monopolised identity, sacred leaders or beliefs, threatened standing, and rewards for conformity. A central warning is that tyranny can begin with ideas people already find attractive. Its attraction cannot be understood only by imagining that followers initially wanted cruelty or recognised the destination.

Better rules can direct ambition toward discovery

Status seeking is also a source of human achievement. Storr’s historical account asks how societies came to reward new knowledge, useful innovation, and demonstrated competence more strongly. His answer includes changes that weakened closed kinship structures and expanded contact among people who were not already bound together by family, inherited rank, or local obligation.

The Republic of Letters is his central example. Scholars communicated through letters, publications, and wider intellectual networks. Standing depended increasingly on original contributions and the judgement of peers. Knowledge could travel beyond a local circle, be contested, and become the basis for further work. A discovery brought recognition, encouraging the effort that made more discovery possible.

The rules mattered as much as the desire to excel. Openness, acknowledgement of intellectual debts, criticism, careful records, reproducible results, and publication helped connect recognition with contribution. Storr contrasts this with settings in which inherited wisdom and obedience are more prestigious than questioning or novelty. The same human appetite can generate different outcomes under different standards.

In his wider narrative, institutional changes allowed commercial and intellectual success to gain influence against older forms of authority. These developments helped create the conditions for the accumulation of knowledge and material improvement. He presents a broad interpretation of complex history, drawing together several sources. It should not be read as proof that status alone caused modern science or economic growth, or that these changes were inevitable.

The general principle is more modest and useful: ambition can be directed toward activities that benefit people beyond the ambitious individual. Recognition for solving problems, sharing what works, and correcting mistakes can make cooperation productive. Recognition for defending an infallible doctrine can do the opposite. The quality of the game depends on whether its celebrated achievements make genuine contributions possible and whether its rules allow those contributions to be evaluated.

Merit depends on opportunity and the modern game distributes both unevenly

A society can praise achievement while making opportunities to demonstrate ability profoundly unequal. Storr discusses racial discrimination, gender barriers, class signals, family resources, and differences in access to education. Officially open competition does not establish that every person starts with comparable knowledge, security, or connections. The unwritten rules of a profession may favour people who learned them long before entering it.

He also treats advantage as multidimensional. Health, temperament, attractiveness, abilities, geography, and family circumstances can affect a person’s prospects alongside social categories. These influences interact differently in different settings. Recognising complexity should not become a reason to dismiss discrimination; it should make explanations of success and failure less complacent.

Modern achievement culture adds another pressure. When personal worth is measured through educational performance, work, appearance, possessions, and constant self-presentation, the opportunities for feeling inadequate multiply. Storr discusses perfectionism and the belief that other people require us to meet exceptionally high standards. A person may be driven by an audience’s imagined expectations even when those expectations have not been clearly stated.

The problems become sharper when effort appears to bring diminishing rewards. Storr describes communities and generations experiencing loss of respected roles, insecure prospects, and exclusion from the status they expected. Material hardship matters, but the accompanying damage to dignity and identity can also shape political anger. Groups interpret the hierarchy differently and blame different people for its unfairness.

His discussion of contemporary political conflict is rooted in the period of the supplied 2021 edition. He argues that rival movements can each perceive real injuries while making simplifying, self-serving claims about the whole system. Minority exclusion and the alienation of disadvantaged majority-group members need not cancel one another. A more useful response examines specific barriers and losses, rather than assuming that one social label tells the entire story of a person’s life. Fairer games need credible opportunities to contribute and receive recognition, not merely repeated assurances that the best people always win.

Build trust offer recognition and keep several identities

Storr ends with seven recommendations for living more intelligently within the status game. His first is to practise warmth, sincerity, and competence. Warmth suggests we will not rely on intimidation; sincerity suggests that our apparent intentions can be trusted; competence makes us valuable. Together, these qualities encourage people to grant respect willingly. They also provide a practical alternative to demanding an elevated position before making a contribution.

The second recommendation is to create small moments of prestige for other people. Everyday obstacles can activate an unnecessary contest: a call-centre exchange, queue, or disagreement starts to feel like a test of whether we will submit. A respectful response can acknowledge effort and preserve the other person’s dignity. It may help resolve the problem, and it avoids adding pointless humiliation when the desired outcome remains unavailable.

Storr emphasises that we have recognition to give. Acknowledging useful work or allowing someone a genuine choice can help them feel valuable rather than coerced. The precise behaviour depends on cultural expectations. The underlying idea is to make cooperation a source of freely enjoyed respect, instead of treating every interaction as a chance to establish dominance. Repeated small exchanges help create our reputation over time.

His third recommendation is to participate in several games, organised by priority. A single all-consuming identity makes a person vulnerable both to manipulation and to collapse if membership or standing is lost. Other communities offer alternative relationships, standards, and perspectives. A person can retain a meaningful life when one audience becomes hostile or one role comes to an end.

This does not require equal investment in every pursuit. Skill and usefulness take sustained attention, so Storr recommends a hierarchy of commitments. One activity can be central while others remain genuinely important. The combination supports depth without making one organisation or belief the owner of the entire self. It also protects the freedom to disagree: questioning a group becomes more possible when it no longer appears to threaten every source of belonging and worth.

Use moral restraint trade-offs originality and awareness

The fourth recommendation is to reduce the range of people we constantly judge. Storr asks readers to pay greater attention to their own conduct and less to the easy rewards of condemning distant strangers. This is especially relevant when a smartphone places endless moral contests within reach. Judgement can feel productive because it supplies recognition, even when it changes little and causes avoidable injury.

Fifth, adopt a trade-off mindset. Complex institutions and social choices usually create both benefits and costs, distributed unevenly among groups. Treating a question as a struggle between wholly virtuous and wholly wicked people makes those effects harder to see. Storr urges us to understand how an outcome might injure an opponent and why their sources of dignity matter to them. Empathy does not mean believing every claim or abandoning the effort to correct injustice. It means recognising human stakes outside our own group’s preferred story.

Sixth, look for useful ways to be different. Perfectionism makes life an attempt to satisfy ever-rising expectations. A distinctive contribution may provide another route to success, particularly when it draws on abilities we can develop well. Storr’s qualification matters: originality works best when it remains useful and does not merely violate the group’s basic standards. Difference by itself is not the same as value.

Seventh, remember that our perception is partly shaped by the stories our minds and communities construct. Notice when a small humiliation becomes a sweeping narrative of heroes and villains. Notice when changing symbols of success begin to feel like permanent truths about personal worth. Storr describes learning to recognise these patterns in himself and finding better games as his life and age changed.

The final implication is that a meaningful life need not culminate in universal recognition or a position beyond criticism. That destination keeps receding because the game does not have a final scoreboard we can possess forever. We can instead choose worthwhile commitments, build real competence, remain open to correction, and offer others the respect we also need. Satisfaction can come from making progress within a life that remains plural and revisable, while understanding why no single victory is likely to end the desire for more.
